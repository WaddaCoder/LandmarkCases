PPC Landmark Cases (Part 1)

ELITE IMPUNITY — SOCIAL MEDIA JUSTICE
1. Raymond Davis Case (2011) — Lahore
Sessions Court Lahore 2011 — released on Diyat after diplomatic immunity dispute. Raymond Davis, an American working at the US Consulate in Lahore, shot and killed two Pakistani men — Mohammad Faheem and Faizan Haider — whom he claimed were attempting to rob him at gunpoint while he was stopped in traffic on Mall Road. A third Pakistani, Mohammad Ibad ur Rehman, was killed when a US Consulate vehicle rushing to Davis’s aid drove the wrong way and struck him. Davis was arrested at the scene. The United States immediately claimed full diplomatic immunity. The case was resolved in March 2011 when the families of the two victims accepted Diyat — blood money reportedly in the region of $2.3 million — and forgave Davis.[cite: 1]
2. Shahzeb Khan Murder Case (2012/2022) — Karachi
ATC-3 Karachi 2013 / SHC 2017-2018 / SC 2022 — Shahrukh Jatoi acquitted. On December 24, 2012, 20-year-old Shahzeb Khan — son of a mid-ranking police officer — was gunned down in Karachi’s Defence Housing Authority. The #JusticeForShahzeb movement on social media forced the criminal justice system to act. The Supreme Court ultimately acquitted all accused in 2022 when counsel argued the accused had no intention to spread terror, therefore ATA did not apply. The death sentence was extinguished through the combination of parental Diyat acceptance and ATA charge removal.[cite: 1]
COMMON INTENTION — S.34 PPC
1. Mahbub Shah v. Emperor (1945)
AIR 1945 PC 118 (Privy Council). The foundational Privy Council authority on S.34 PPC. Common intention implies a pre-arranged plan and acting in concert pursuant to that plan. Mere presence at the scene of a crime, even with knowledge that a crime is being committed, does not by itself establish common intention. The meeting of minds must precede the act.[cite: 1]
2. Basharat v. The State (2023)
2023 SCMR 975. The most recent Supreme Court authority on S.34 in the app. Where a person is present throughout a killing but their role is ambiguous — neither clearly active participant nor clearly innocent bystander — what degree of participation is required? The Supreme Court's analysis distinguishes between participation that advances the common intention and presence that does not constitute furtherance.[cite: 1]
3. Moazzam Abbas v. The State (2024)
2024 MLD 1055. Authority on the bedrock principle that mere presence at the scene of a crime is insufficient to establish criminal liability under S.34. Presence raises an inference. It does not create an irrebuttable presumption of guilt.[cite: 1]
MOB VIOLENCE — COMMON INTENTION IN PRACTICE
1. Youhanabad Mob Lynching (2015) — Lahore
ATC Lahore 2015. On 15 March 2015, suicide bombers attacked two churches. In the aftermath, a mob from the Christian community captured two Muslim men they suspected of involvement and burned them alive. Both men had no connection to the bombings. The courts had to decide whether witnessing a bombing constitutes “grave and sudden provocation” in the legal sense for mob members who responded with killing.[cite: 1]
2. Priyantha Kumara Lynching (2021) — Sialkot
ATC Sialkot 2021/2022 — 88 persons convicted. Priyantha Kumara Diyawadana, a Sri Lankan national, was accused by workers of desecrating posters bearing Islamic scripture. A mob of hundreds tortured and killed him. The defining modern illustration of S.34 applied to mob violence. The mob had no single leader and no pre-written plan. Yet the Mahbub Shah conception of common intention applied to every member who actively participated.[cite: 1]
STATE VIOLENCE — ACCOUNTABILITY AND IMPUNITY
1. Murtaza Bhutto Murder Case (1996) — Karachi
FIR 443/1996, Sessions Court Karachi — all 18 accused acquitted. Mir Murtaza Bhutto, along with his close aide and six comrades, was shot dead in an alleged police encounter. The court acquitted all 18 policemen. The legal result: eight men were killed on a public street in Karachi, and nobody was convicted of anything.[cite: 1]
2. Model Town Massacre (2014) — Lahore
On June 17, 2014, Punjab Police opened live fire on PAT workers and supporters. Fourteen people were killed. A one-judge judicial commission held the Punjab government “directly responsible” for the bloodshed. The Lahore High Court commission’s finding that the orders were unlawful directly engages the S.76 defence.[cite: 1]
GENERAL EXCEPTIONS — S.96-106 PPC
Mashal Khan v. The State (1988)
PLD 1988 SC 25. The Supreme Court's defining authority on the proportionality requirement in private defence — the 'Golden Scales' rule. Force used in exercise of the right of private defence must be weighed on golden scales against the force threatened.[cite: 1]
ABETMENT — S.107-109 PPC
1. ZA Bhutto v. State (1979)
PLD 1979 SC 53. Bhutto was convicted not as the person who pulled the trigger but as the person who instigated and directed the Federal Security Force operatives who did. The case is the most senior-level application of S.107/109 in Pakistani legal history.[cite: 1]
2. Mumtaz Qadri (2016)
PLD 2016 SC 17. The case drew a crucial line: where does protected religious speech end and criminal instigation begin? The test is whether the words were calculated to produce the commission of an offence in a specific context.[cite: 1]
BLASPHEMY — S.295-B / 295-C PPC
1. Mumtaz Qadri v. State (2016)
PLD 2016 SC 17. The Supreme Court's rejection of Qadri's defence established conclusively that S.295-C enforcement is the state's exclusive domain. No private citizen, regardless of religious conviction, has standing to punish alleged blasphemy.[cite: 1]
2. Asia Bibi v. State (2019)
PLD 2019 SC 64. The judgment identified five red flags present in the case: prior dispute, heated confrontation, sole testimony without corroboration, inconsistent reporting, and community pressure. These established that allegations are frequently fabricated as instruments of personal vendetta.[cite: 1]
3. Rizwan Haider (2014)
Police officer Faraz Naveed killed a detainee — a Shia Muslim with a history of mental instability — with an axe inside a police station in Gujrat. This case is distinct because the killing was committed by a law enforcement officer inside a place of custody.[cite: 1]
MURDER — S.299-302 PPC
1. ZA Bhutto v. State (1979)
PLD 1979 SC 53. The trial and execution of Prime Minister Zulfikar Ali Bhutto for the murder of Nawab Muhammad Ahmed Khan Kasuri. The Supreme Court confirmed the death sentence 4-3.[cite: 1]
2. Sher Khan v. The State (1991)
1991 SCMR 241. The defining Supreme Court authority on S.300 Proviso 4 — the sudden fight exclusion. A manufactured quarrel designed to create an opportunity to kill does not attract Proviso 4.[cite: 1]
3. Mumtaz Qadri v. State (2016)
PLD 2016 SC 17. The most politically significant criminal case of 21st-century Pakistan. Qadri assassinated Governor Taseer in January 2011. The Supreme Court rejected every defence advanced.[cite: 1]
4. Qandeel Baloch / S.311 Test Case
State v. Muhammad Waseem (2019/2022). Qandeel Baloch was strangled by her brother Muhammad Waseem in July 2016. The parents formally forgave him. The Lahore High Court applied S.311 and held the honour killing character meant parental forgiveness could not save him.[cite: 1]
5. Mst. Safia Bano v. Home Department (2021)
PLD 2021 SC 488. Five-member Supreme Court bench held that no death sentence shall be carried out if a condemned prisoner, due to mental illness, is found unable to comprehend the rationale and reason behind their punishment.[cite: 1]
6. Sara Inam Murder Case (2022/2024)
State v. Shahnawaz Amir. Sara Inam was murdered by her husband Shahnawaz Amir. Shahnawaz was sentenced to death by the sessions court and fined Rs1 million. The case is now before the Islamabad High Court on appeal.[cite: 1]
TERRORISM — ATA INTERSECTIONS WITH PPC
1. APS Peshawar Attack (2014)
Pakistani Taliban gunmen stormed the Army Public School in Peshawar and killed 132 schoolchildren and 9 staff members[cite: 1]. The attack triggered the National Action Plan, the establishment of military courts, and the lifting of the moratorium on the death penalty[cite: 1].
2. Lahore Data Darbar Bombing (2010)
ATC Lahore proceedings 2010-2011. Two suicide bombers detonated simultaneously in the packed shrine, killing 42 people[cite: 1]. The case tests the limits of circumstantial evidence in conspiracy prosecution when the bombers themselves die in the attack[cite: 1].
ACID ATTACKS — CRIMINAL LAW AND REFORM
1. Fakhra Younus (2000)
Karachi 2000. Fakhra Younus was attacked with acid by her estranged husband Bilal Khar[cite: 1]. He was acquitted in 2003 when four eyewitnesses refused to identify him[cite: 1]. After twelve years and 39 surgeries, she jumped from her apartment, leaving a note blaming “the silence of law”[cite: 1].
RAPE — S.375-376 PPC
1. Safia Bibi v. State (1985)
1985 PLD FSC 120. A nearly blind young woman was raped by her employer and his son[cite: 1]. The Zina-bil-jabr provision required four male Muslim witnesses to the actual act of penetration for conviction, a standard that could not be met[cite: 1]. Safia Bibi was sentenced to public lashes and imprisonment[cite: 1].
2. Veena Hayat (1991)
Karachi, November 1991. Veena Hayat was gang-raped inside her own home in Karachi[cite: 1]. She was assaulted and tortured for 12 hours[cite: 1]. This remains the only case in the log where rape was allegedly deployed as a direct instrument of political intimidation[cite: 1].
3. Mukhtaran Mai v. State (2005/2011)
In June 2002, Mukhtaran Mai was gang-raped on the orders of a village panchayat as collective punishment[cite: 1]. The legal journey resulted in acquittals despite an internationally documented fact pattern[cite: 1].
4. Two-Finger Test Abolition (2021)
PLD 2021 SC 600. The Supreme Court declared the two-finger test inadmissible on the grounds of scientific unreliability and violation of the complainant's fundamental rights under Articles 9 and 14 of the Constitution[cite: 1].
CHILD PROTECTION — CRIMINAL LAW AND REFORM
1. Kasur Child Abuse Scandal (2015)
A paedophile ring in Husain Khanwala village systematically abused hundreds of children and used recordings for blackmail[cite: 1]. The legal proceedings were deeply unsatisfactory; a small number of accused were convicted while the network largely escaped[cite: 1].
2. Zainab Ansari Murder (2018)
Seven-year-old Zainab Ansari was kidnapped, raped, and murdered[cite: 1]. Imran Ali was arrested after DNA evidence matched him to the crime[cite: 1]. He confessed to the rape and murder of eight other children in the same area[cite: 1].
THEFT — S.378 PPC
1. Muhammad Riaz v. The State (1966)
PLD 1966 Lah. 379. Lahore High Court authority on the distinction between honest and dishonest intention[cite: 1]. If the accused genuinely believed they had a legal right to the property, the mental element of S.378 fails[cite: 1].
2. Atta Ullah Khan v. The State (1991)
PLJ 1991 Cr.C. Lah. 353. Lahore High Court's authority on the double conviction rule[cite: 1]. An accused cannot be convicted simultaneously under S.382 PPC (theft in a dwelling) and S.411 PPC (receiving stolen property) for the same goods[cite: 1].
3. Recent Jurisprudence on Stolen Property Recovery (2025)
2025 PCrLJ 517 [Sindh]. The central question: does recovery of stolen property short-circuit the element-by-element S.378 analysis? The case holds that recovery raises a presumption but does not conclusively establish dishonest intention[cite: 1].
ROBBERY — S.390 PPC
1. Muhammad Shafi v. The State (1959)
PLD 1959 Kar. 648. The Karachi High Court's authority on the threshold question in Robbery: at what point does Theft escalate to Robbery? The force must be applied in order to the taking, during the taking, or immediately after to retain what was taken[cite: 1].
2. Ghulam Mohiuddin alias Haji Babu v. The State (2014)
2014 SCMR 1034. Supreme Court authority on vicarious liability in Dacoity and armed robbery under S.392/395 read with S.34[cite: 1]. The Supreme Court's analysis examines the scope of common intention in the robbery context[cite: 1].
